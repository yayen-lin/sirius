This section evaluates the exact join (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-exact}) and the approximate join (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-approx}) against GPU and CPU vector-search libraries on standard vector-search benchmark sets, including data larger than GPU memory; then the vector join inside complex SQL statements against DuckDB, a GPU database with vector operators and a GPU pipeline (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-sql}); then semantic deduplication against a production pipeline (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-selfjoin}); and finally the planner's decisions (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-planner}) and the join's limitations (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-limits}).

\paragraph{Hardware and systems}
Every experiment runs on an NVIDIA RTX A5000 (24\,GB, sm\_86, capped at 200\,W by the host) with 24 CPU cores and 240\,GB of host memory; the deduplication comparison uses a second machine of the same type.
The baselines:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
    \item \emph{Exact search on the GPU}: FAISS-GPU 1.14.1's flat search~\cite{JohnsonFaissGPU21} and cuVS 26.02's fp32 brute force~\cite{NvidiaCuVS26}; \emph{on the CPU}: FAISS's flat search (MKL build, 24 threads), the same GEMM on the host.
    \item \emph{Approximate search on the GPU}: five indexes, FAISS-GPU's IVF, cuVS's IVF-PQ and CAGRA (26.02), and cuVS's IVF-SQ and IVF-RaBitQ (26.08)~\cite{OotomoCAGRA24, ShiIVFRaBitQ26}; \emph{on the CPU}: DuckDB's HNSW index join~\cite{DuckDBVSSBlog24}, the one CPU system that answers an approximate join from SQL.
    \item \emph{SQL}: stock DuckDB on 24 threads, MaxVec~\cite{MaxVec26}, and a pipeline of DuckDB and cuVS (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-sql}); \emph{deduplication}: NeMo Curator~\cite{NeMoCurator}.
\end{itemize}
Each index is built over a grid of its build parameters and searched over its search parameters, and for each recall target we take the configuration that is cheapest under the accounting below; Sirius's partition counts from 256 to 16\,384 are swept the same way, and Section~\ref{subsec:eval-planner} shows that the planner's own pick is within 10\,\% of the sweep.
Table~\ref{tab:datasets} lists the datasets, standard vector-search benchmark sets with their public queries: SIFT1M, GIST1M and Deep10M fit in GPU memory; SIFT100M and Deep100M do not, and there the libraries build from host memory and search on the device, which every index can do because it stores compressed vectors (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-approx} caps the GPU to show the limit of that).

\paragraph{Accounting}
A vector join builds what it needs inside the statement, so every system is charged its build: for Sirius the k-means fit, the code or partition build and the join; for a library the index build and the search (FAISS's flat search has none).
Loading from storage and process start-up are excluded for every system, except in the deduplication comparison, which times both systems from process start.
Sirius's times are medians over three to five processes, in each the median of the join's runs after the first; FAISS's flat search reports its best of three runs after a warm-up, and each index configuration is built once and searched once; at 100M rows FAISS's flat search pages the rows through the GPU with its own \texttt{knn\_gpu} loop.
Recall is recall@10 against the published ground truth of the 10k public queries, and $k{=}10$ except where said.

\subsection{Exact Join}\label{subsec:eval-exact}

\input{figs-eval/fig_exact}

Figure~\ref{fig:exact} compares the exact join with exact search at 10k probe rows (1k on GIST1M, which has no more public queries).
On the three sets that fit in GPU memory, Sirius is 6.5--6.9$\times$ faster than the second-fastest system, FAISS-GPU's flat search, on SIFT1M and Deep10M, and 2.7$\times$ on GIST1M, where its code build weighs more against 1k probe rows.
The margin comes from never writing a distance and from multiplying 1- or 2-byte codes, which the ablation below separates.
On the two 100M sets, where the rows start in host memory, Sirius is 5.9--7.6$\times$ faster, with the build (int8 codes on Deep100M, where halving the bytes that cross PCIe pays) about half of its time; cuVS's brute force needs the rows on the device and does not run there.
FAISS's flat search on 24 CPU cores is 126--177$\times$ slower than Sirius, except on GIST1M (32$\times$), whose 960-wide rows let the CPU's GEMM run near its peak; DuckDB, which evaluates the same \texttt{LATERAL} statement one pair at a time, is three orders of magnitude slower; the same statement in Sirius is 5.8$\times$ faster than FAISS-GPU on Deep10M.
Every answer in Figures~\ref{fig:exact} to~\ref{fig:ablation} is exact: for every probe row, no distance in Sirius's answer exceeds the corresponding distance in the published ground truth, recomputed in fp64, and ids differ only between rows at equal distance.

From 1k to 1M probe rows on Deep10M every exact system's time grows linearly, as an exact join's must, and Sirius's margin over FAISS-GPU settles at about 7$\times$ from 10k on (3.6$\times$ at 1k, where the code build is a third of its time); at 1M probe rows the exact join takes 41\,s, and the approximate join of Section~\ref{subsec:eval-approx} takes over.

\paragraph{Larger $k$}
Because the seed's sample grows with $k$ (Section~\ref{subsec:exact-bound}), the margin holds beyond $k{=}10$: on Deep10M the exact join is 6.7$\times$ faster than FAISS-GPU's flat search at $k{=}100$ and 5.4$\times$ at $k{=}1\,000$, exact up to $k{=}2\,048$, the largest we ran.
On the 1M-row sets it narrows to 1.3--1.8$\times$ at $k{=}1\,000$, where returning the result rows, which grow with $k$, takes most of Sirius's time.

\paragraph{Where the speed comes from}\label{subsec:eval-ablations}

\input{figs-eval/fig_ablation}

\yxy{make this paragraph more intuitive; hard to read right now}
Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} adds the ideas of Section~\ref{sec:exact-join} one at a time on Deep10M, 10k probe rows.
The starting point is what a search library does: compute every distance, write it, and select each row's top-$k$ from what was written.
That takes 3.0\,s, and the same in fp16 (2.9\,s), because the time goes to writing and re-reading $10^{11}$ distances, not to the multiplication.
Filtering inside the GEMM under the bound removes that traffic: on the fp32 rows themselves, multiplied on the tensor cores' fp32 path with the bound widened for its rounding and the survivors re-scored, the join takes 0.75\,s, 4.0$\times$ faster.
The codes then buy what the tensor cores give narrower types: fp16 another 1.9$\times$, at twice the rate on half the bytes, and int8 another 1.6$\times$, with the kernel at about 36\,\% of the rate a plain int8 GEMM reaches on this GPU.
The filter is worth nothing without its bound: unseeded, the fp16 filter lets almost every pair through and takes 3.45\,s, slower than writing the distances; the 1\,024-row seed brings it to 1.08\,s and lowering the bound between pieces to the 0.39\,s of the figure.
The approximate join reuses the kernel and gains the same 2.8$\times$ from the filter at 1M probe rows (2.0 against 5.7\,s with fp16 partitions).
int8 is not the default because one scale per column fails on skewed data: with Deep10M's first component scaled by 300, so many pairs pass the widened int8 limit that the join takes 8.8\,s against 0.92\,s with fp16 (the rotation still helps: at a scale of 20 it keeps int8 17$\times$ faster than without it).

\input{figs-eval/tab_margins}

Two effects explain the shape of the curves.
At 10k probe rows the build decides: on the 100M sets Sirius builds its partitions faster than any library builds its index, and CAGRA's graph build is itself a neighbor search over every build row, which a join of 10k probe rows does not need.
As the probe side grows the search decides: an IVF index reads each list once for every probe row that visits it, whereas Sirius reads each partition once per probe block (Section~\ref{subsec:approx-join}).
The same effect makes large joins affordable: the dashed line in Figure~\ref{fig:probes}a is Sirius's exact join, and at 1M probe rows the approximate join takes 2.6\,s against its 41\,s.

\paragraph{An index that already exists}
\input{figs-eval/fig_prebuilt}
A vector database would hold a prebuilt index and run the join as an index nested-loop join over it, so Figure~\ref{fig:prebuilt} separates the build from the search on Deep100M, each system at the configuration whose search is fastest.
The indexes that search fastest take 58 to 775\,s to build, against 10 to 24\,s for the cheapest IVF indexes Figure~\ref{fig:probes} charges and 8.5\,s for Sirius's partitions, and with the build left out CAGRA's search is the fastest at 10k probe rows, 2.7$\times$ faster than the join, and level with it at 1M (5.1 against 5.0\,s); every IVF index stays at least 6$\times$ slower than the join at 1M, for the reason above.
The graph repays its build only when the same build side serves thousands of small statements, about 10\,000 of 10k probe rows on Deep100M, which is the search workload of Section~\ref{sec:gpu-vector-join}, not the join workload: each join statement brings its own probe side and, often, its own build side.
At 1M probe rows, and on Deep10M at any size, the join's own search already matches the graph's, so the graph never repays.
Choosing between the two from what the catalog holds is future work (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).

\subsection{Vector Join in Complex SQL Queries}\label{subsec:eval-sql}

\input{figs-eval/lst_vech}
\input{figs-eval/fig_sql}

Vec-H extends TPC-H at scale factor 1 with tables of product reviews and product images, with embeddings of 1\,024 and 1\,152 dimensions~\cite{MaxVec26}; our statements join the 2.4M reviews and the 196\,824 main product images.
Its own queries search with one query vector, so we wrote seven join statements over its tables.
QF, QJ and QE put a vector join inside a TPC-H plan: reviews that echo a complaint, brands with similar reviews, and products for unhappy buyers.
J1--J3 join two sets of embeddings: near-duplicate images, the reviews of two supplier groups, and recommendations for 94k reviews.
QB joins images on both a similarity and a 1\,\% price band.
Each statement runs unchanged in DuckDB and in Sirius, and Sirius rewrites every one into its join; Figure~\ref{fig:vech-sql} shows one of them.
Here every system is warm: Sirius's codes were built by an earlier statement, the pipeline below holds the build side on the GPU, and MaxVec holds its flat index.

Sirius is the fastest system on every statement, against every system that runs it (Figure~\ref{fig:sql}a).
The closest is a pipeline that runs the probe side in DuckDB, the join in cuVS with the build side already on the GPU, and the rest in DuckDB again; Sirius is at least 3.4$\times$ faster on the four top-$k$ statements, with neither side charged a build and without charging the pipeline the 26\,s it takes to export the build side from DuckDB.
Both run an exact join on the GPU, so much of the margin is the hand-off between systems: on J2, the smallest statement, DuckDB's export of the two inputs takes 0.46 of the pipeline's 0.50\,s.
MaxVec, a GPU database with vector-search operators, keeps the tables in host memory on our 24\,GB GPU (its own evaluation uses GPUs with four times the memory) and is 3.4--3.6$\times$ slower on the two statements its GPU path supports, QJ and QE.
Its GPU indexes have no range search (QF, J1) and its GPU plan rejects the aggregates of J2 and J3, so it runs those four on its CPU, up to 72$\times$ slower.

Stock DuckDB on 24 CPU threads is three to four orders of magnitude slower on most statements; the exceptions are J2, whose join is small (48$\times$), and QB, where DuckDB joins on the price band first (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-planner}).
Figure~\ref{fig:sql}b shows why: on the five statements where DuckDB's plan keeps the vector join separate, the join is 97 to 100\,\% of its time, whereas the subqueries that feed the join take milliseconds in both systems.
The answers agree with DuckDB's up to fp32 rounding: on all 1\,744 probe rows of QJ the top-5 similarities match within $10^{-5}$, and 85 rows pick other rows at equal similarity.
Both systems compare a similarity with a threshold in fp32, so a pair within rounding of it may fall on either side: Sirius keeps a few pairs of QB at 0.89999982, which DuckDB drops.

\subsection{Deduplication against NeMo Curator}\label{subsec:eval-selfjoin}

\input{figs-eval/fig_dedup}

Semantic deduplication removes a row whose most similar other row passes a cosine threshold, a standard step in cleaning training data~\cite{AbbasSemDeDup23}.
NeMo Curator, NVIDIA's pipeline for it~\cite{NeMoCurator}, clusters the rows with k-means and compares rows within each cluster on the GPU; Sirius runs the rule, through its table function, as one threshold self-join whose pairs are grouped to each row's most similar earlier row.
We use the 5.87M CLIP embeddings of DataComp-small, 18\,GB in fp32 on a 24\,GB GPU, time each system from process start to the list of rows to remove, and score both against the exact removal set computed in fp64.

Sirius is 4.6$\times$ faster and finds more of the duplicates (Figure~\ref{fig:dedup}).
Most of the gap is NeMo Curator's framework: starting its process and its Ray cluster and orchestrating actors between its stages take 49\,s.
Of the 51\,s its stages themselves take, 31\,s is unified-memory paging, because the 18\,GB table does not fit on the device beside its index; without the paging its work takes about as long as Sirius's, 19 to 21\,s.
What the same work buys differs: Sirius finds 0.997 of the rows to remove and NeMo Curator 0.959, because NeMo Curator compares rows only within a cluster and misses pairs that straddle clusters; its configuration that finds the most (0.964) takes 126.5\,s.
Folding the best match into the join itself (Section~\ref{subsec:integration-plan}), so that only the pairs that can still win are re-scored, brings Sirius to 13.5\,s and the margin to 7.4$\times$.

\subsection{Planner Decisions}\label{subsec:eval-planner}

\input{figs-eval/fig_planner}

The planner makes three decisions (Section~\ref{subsec:integration-plan}): whether an exact join builds its codes or joins the fp32 rows directly, how many partitions an approximate join builds, and whether a vector join or a band join goes first.
Figure~\ref{fig:planner} runs the first two against the plan the planner rejected, for the first statement in a process, where a wrong choice costs the most.
The planner picks the faster plan in 6 of 9 cases, and its misses cost at most 15\,\%.
Building codes pays from 1k probe rows on Deep10M; the two misses are SIFT1M at 1k probe rows and Vec-H's QJ, where the codes make the first statement 7 to 15\,\% slower but every later statement over 3$\times$ faster, because they stay for the session.
Among the partition counts that reach recall 0.96, the cost model picks the fastest at 10k probe rows and one that is 11\,\% slower than the fastest at 1M.
The join-order decision is a different kind: on QB, making the similarity predicate the vector join's own is about 90$\times$ faster than DuckDB's band-first plan, which turns the similarity into a filter after the band join and therefore runs on the CPU.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{subsec:eval-limits}

\yxy{these sound like engineering limitations. Maybe remove this section?}\duke{cut or keep: your call; the blind review called this the most credible paragraph.}
The first join in a process costs Sirius about 0.16\,s more than later ones.
Charged to Sirius alone, this lowers its margin over FAISS-GPU at 10k probe rows to 4.7$\times$ on Deep10M and 1.5$\times$ on SIFT1M, and makes FAISS-GPU faster on GIST1M's 1k probe rows (0.8$\times$).
Beyond that, the join's reach is narrower than a library's.
It runs on one GPU.
Its int8 codes cover at most 256 dimensions, so wider columns use fp16.
A relational filter written outside the join's subquery is not pushed through the join and runs after it: with a filter that keeps 0.06\,\% of the Vec-H reviews, that is 1.9$\times$ slower than filtering first.
Ordering by inner product is not rewritten, so inner-product search over unnormalized embeddings falls back to DuckDB, as do \texttt{arg\_max} over the join's output, ordering by largest distance, and a predicate that asks for dissimilar pairs; the best-match join (Section~\ref{subsec:integration-plan}) is reached only through the table function.
Finally, codes and partitions are rebuilt, not maintained, when a table changes.
